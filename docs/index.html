Predicting Content Decline for Human-Reviewed SEO Decision Support

A validation-focused study of whether historical search and engagement signals can help prioritize content pages for human review before a later decline in clicks.

Krisha Merja · Machine Learning Engineering

Abstract Problem Data Methodology Results Limitations Recommendations Reproducibility Credit

Abstract

This study asks whether historical search and engagement signals can help identify content pages that may experience a later decline in clicks, so human reviewers can decide what to inspect first.

Using the March 2026 FlyRank ML Internship dataset, I constructed an early feature window and a later outcome window and trained a Logistic Regression model using five search and engagement features.

Evaluation used a client-grouped split so that clients appearing in the validation set did not also appear in training, together with a feature leakage audit.

On this grouped validation design, the model measured Precision@50 of 0.70, compared with 0.32 for the Week-4 rule baseline on the same evaluation setting.

The result is interpreted as directional evidence for human-reviewed decision support, not as proof of causal content decline or as justification for automatic content changes.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams can manage many pages at once, but review time is limited. The practical problem is therefore not simply whether a page's performance changes, but which pages should a human inspect first.

This project investigates whether signals already available from search and engagement performance can provide a useful ranking of pages that may later experience lower click activity.

Research question: Can historical search and engagement signals help identify content pages that may experience a later decline in clicks, so human reviewers can prioritize inspection?

The intended use is prioritization. A high model score is not treated as proof that a page is poor, outdated, or responsible for a traffic change. Instead, the score provides one measured signal that can support a human review workflow.

3. Methodology

Model

I used Logistic Regression as an interpretable predictive model.

Features

  • Impressions
  • Clicks
  • Average position
  • Pageviews
  • Engaged sessions

Outcome label

The target label represents whether the page's later average daily clicks were lower than its early average daily clicks.

The observed decline rate in the prepared modeling data was approximately 36.43%.

Validation design

The final evaluation used a grouped-by-client split. Training contained 28,227 rows and the held-out evaluation group contained 1,167 rows. Client overlap between training and evaluation was zero.

This is stricter than a random row split because records associated with the same client cannot appear on both sides of the final validation.

Baseline

Model performance was compared with the Week-4 rule baseline using the same evaluation framing. Precision@50 was used because the practical task is to prioritize a small review queue rather than automatically classify every page.

Leakage audit

The final predictive feature set contained no identified forbidden predictive inputs. Features were calculated from the early window, while the outcome was measured from the later window.

Validation check: client overlap = 0 · forbidden predictive inputs = 0 · feature window precedes outcome window.

4. Results

Under the client-grouped validation design, Logistic Regression measured a Precision@50 of 0.70. The Week-4 rule baseline measured 0.32 under the comparison used in the capstone.

Validation audit

An earlier random split produced Precision@50 of approximately 0.74 and contained client overlap. After changing to the stricter client-grouped split, Precision@50 measured 0.70 with zero client overlap.

The lower grouped result is the value used for the final claim because it better reflects evaluation on clients separated from the training set.

Error inspection

The grouped validation audit observed both false positives and false negatives, including 88 false positives and 321 false negatives in the audited prediction setting. These errors reinforce that the scores should support review rather than trigger automatic content changes.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

Limitation Interpretation
Single-month study March 2026 may not represent other periods.
Generalisation Performance on new populations and future periods remains uncertain.
Proxy outcome A decline in clicks does not by itself establish a decline in content quality.
Limited feature set Important contextual factors may not be represented by the five predictive features.
Causality Predictive association does not establish what caused a performance change.
Human context Model scores require human review before a content decision.
The evidence supports an observed, measured, directional, human-reviewed decision-support claim. It does not support guaranteed future performance or causal claims.

Reason codes

Recommendations are accompanied by reason codes such as HIGH_DECLINE_SCORE_HIGH_VIS and MODERATE_DECLINE_SCORE. These communicate why an item entered a review tier without presenting the model score as proof that a content change is necessary.

Human review

Before acting, a reviewer should consider:

  • traffic evidence,
  • ranking and search intent,
  • content freshness and factual accuracy,
  • seasonality,
  • business value, and
  • the cost of editing.

What should not be automated

  • Automatic content rewriting
  • Automatic deletion or publishing
  • Automatic metadata changes
  • Treating a model score as proof of decline
  • Refreshing content solely because of a model score
  • Acting without human context review

Monitoring

The playbook uses grouped Precision@50 of 0.70 as its reference validation result. Monitoring should consider changes in model precision, feature distributions, decline base rate, new-client behaviour, human-review quality, and data-pipeline definitions.

A measured Precision@50 below 0.60 is used as an investigation trigger in the playbook. Retraining is a human-reviewed decision rather than an automatic response.
